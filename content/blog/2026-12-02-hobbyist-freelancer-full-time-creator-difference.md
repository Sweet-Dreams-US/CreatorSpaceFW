People who make things for a living, and people who make things for fun, often use the same words for themselves. Someone who sells a few prints at a fair, someone who takes client video jobs on weekends and someone who runs a full creative business can all say "I am a creator." The word fits all three. The paths are different, though, and knowing which one you are on makes it easier to decide what to do next.

This article explains the three common paths, what separates them and how people move from one to another.

## Who counts as a creator

A 2022 report on the creator economy, commissioned by Meta and written by Richard Florida, defines creators as people "who use digital technology to make and publish unique creative content audiences can access and respond to." Florida is the author of *The Rise of the Creative Class*.

That definition is wide. It covers a photographer posting work online, a musician releasing tracks, a writer publishing essays and a designer sharing projects. It says nothing about money. A creator is defined by making and sharing, not by earning.

:::note Worth knowing
The report cites estimates of 30 to 85 million creators in the United States and about 300 million worldwide, drawing on Edelman Data & Intelligence (2022).
:::

Inside that wide group, three paths are worth separating.

## The hobbyist

A hobbyist creates because they want to. Income, if any, is a side effect. The same report notes that many creators are passionate hobbyists, and that most creators are driven by intrinsic rewards. It says many are motivated by the desire to make an impact rather than by money, fame or follower counts.

For a hobbyist, the work has no client, no deadline set by someone else and no price to defend. That freedom is the point. A hobbyist can try a new style, abandon a project or make work no one asked for.

Being a hobbyist is not a stage you are required to leave. Many people stay here for years because they like it, and because turning a hobby into a business changes how it feels. If making things is what you do to rest, think carefully before attaching deadlines and invoices to it.

What a hobbyist often wants from a community is feedback, company and a place to share work. Skills practice and meeting others who care about the same craft matter more than finding clients.

## The freelancer

A freelancer sells their creative skills to clients, usually project by project. They may have a day job, they may freelance part of the time or it may be their main income. What defines the path is that someone else is paying for the work and has a say in what it looks like.

That brings new requirements. A freelancer needs to find clients, agree on what a project includes, set a price, deliver on time and handle payment. The creative skill is the same one a hobbyist has. The additional skills are business skills: communicating clearly, writing down the scope of a job and following up.

Freelance work often means making what the client needs instead of what you would choose. Some creators find that satisfying, because a brief is a problem to solve. Others find it drains the enjoyment they had when the work was their own.

A freelancer can be part time or full time. Plenty of people freelance on top of other work for years, which is a normal way to build a client list before relying on it.

What a freelancer often wants from a community is referrals, collaborators who fill gaps in their skills and a way for clients to find them.

## The full-time creator

A full-time creator earns their living from creative work. The report notes that more and more people are earning a living by creating content, and describes a hope for a "creator middle class," in which more of the creators who want to can make a decent living.

Full-time creators may be freelancers, may sell their own work, may earn from audiences or may combine several sources. What matters is that the creative work has to cover the bills. That changes decisions. Time spent on admin, marketing, taxes and planning is part of the job, not a distraction from it. Income that is uneven from month to month requires a plan for the slow months.

Full-time creators also carry the risk themselves. There is no employer to provide a steady paycheck, and income can change quickly.

What a full-time creator often wants from a community is steady collaborators, a network that sends work their way and others who understand the realities of the path.

## How people move between paths

The three paths are not a ladder. People move in every direction. A full-time creator may go back to part-time work to protect the joy of making things. A hobbyist may take on one client and find they like it. A freelancer may have a good year, then a slow one, and adjust.

Some questions help you work out where you are and where you want to be:

1. Why do I make this work? Is it for enjoyment, for income or for both?
2. How much of my income do I want to come from it? None, some or all?
3. How do I feel when someone else tells me what to change? Does it feel like a puzzle or like a loss?
4. How would I handle a month with no income from it?
5. Do I have the skills to find and keep clients, or would I want help with those?

There is no right answer. The answers show which path suits your situation now. They can change as your life does.

## Why the difference matters when you look for help

Advice for full-time creators does not fit a hobbyist, and advice for hobbyists can mislead a freelancer. Someone who tells you to raise your rates assumes you have clients. Someone who tells you to do it for love assumes you do not need the income. When you ask for help, say which path you are on.

The same goes for finding people to work with. A hobbyist looking for a collaborator wants someone with similar interests. A freelancer looking for a collaborator wants someone who can deliver on a client deadline. Being clear about this saves both sides time.

## The short version

A hobbyist makes things for enjoyment, a freelancer sells creative skills to clients and a full-time creator lives on creative income. All three are creators, by the definition used in the Meta report, and people move between the paths as their goals change. Decide what you want from your work, be honest about the business skills it requires and look for people who are on the same path or who have walked it already.
