If you make things and share them online, you have probably seen headlines that put the creator economy in the hundreds of billions of dollars. You may also have seen a much smaller number for the same thing. Both can come from serious research. They differ because each one decides in its own way what counts as the creator economy.

This article looks at two published estimates, one from Goldman Sachs Research and one from Citi as reported by the NZ Herald. It explains what each one says, why they do not match, and what a working creator can take from them.

## The Goldman Sachs estimate

Goldman Sachs Research published its view on April 19, 2023. It put the total addressable market of the creator economy at about $250 billion at that time. It expected that figure to roughly double to $480 billion by 2027.

Goldman's article also gives a count of people. It says there are about 50 million global creators, and it expects that number to keep growing at a rate of 10 to 20 percent a year, compounded, over the following five years.

Two more numbers from the same piece are useful. About 4 percent of global creators earn more than $100,000 a year, and Goldman expects that share to stay steady as the whole field expands. And survey data in the article puts brand deals as the main source of income, at about 70 percent.

Goldman names two things it expects to drive growth: spending on influencer marketing, and platform payouts from advertising on short-form video.

## The Citi estimate

The NZ Herald reports a different set of figures from Citi. Citi counts more than 120 million content creators worldwide. It puts revenue at roughly $60 billion at the time of the report, with a projection of $75 billion by 2024.

The article also says Citi leaves out social media platforms such as Facebook, Instagram and TikTok when it sizes the field. That one choice explains a large part of the difference. Goldman's figure is a total addressable market. Citi's figure is revenue, with the platforms removed.

## Why the numbers do not match

Put the two sets of numbers side by side and the gaps are large. Goldman says about 50 million creators. Citi says more than 120 million. Goldman says $250 billion. Citi says about $60 billion.

There are three reasons a reader should keep in mind.

The first is the definition of a creator. Goldman divides creators into amateurs and professionals, and the NZ Herald reports that Goldman treats 96 percent of its 50 million creators as amateurs and 4 percent as professionals earning over $100,000 a year. Citi counts a much larger group, so it must be using a wider or different test for who qualifies.

The second is what is measured. A total addressable market describes the money that could be reached by the field. Revenue describes money actually collected. These are different quantities, and one is usually much larger than the other.

The third is what is left out. Citi excludes the social platforms, while Goldman's description of growth drivers includes platform payouts from short-form video advertising. If one estimate counts platform money and the other does not, the totals will differ even if both are careful.

## What the estimates say about income

The size of an industry is not the same as the size of an individual income. Goldman's 4 percent figure for creators earning over $100,000 means that most creators in its count earn less than that. The NZ Herald headline is itself a caution to young creators about giving up a day job.

Goldman's survey data on brand deals points to another practical fact: if about 70 percent of income comes from brand deals, then relationships with the businesses that pay for work matter a great deal. For a local creator, that can start with the businesses nearby who need photos, video, writing, music or design.

## How to read a big number

When you see a figure for the creator economy, ask four questions. Who published it? What date was it published? What does it count as a creator? Does it measure potential money or collected revenue? An answer to those questions tells you more than the number itself.

Dates matter too. The Goldman figures above come from April 2023, and the projections look ahead to 2027. A forecast is an estimate of what may happen, and the people who made it say so. Treat it as a reference point for planning rather than a promise.

## A practical takeaway

The two estimates agree on one direction: a large and growing number of people make things online, and money flows to the field through brand work and platform payouts. They disagree on the exact totals because they measure different things.

For someone who is making work today, the numbers are less useful as a promise of income than as a reason to focus on skills and connections. Learn from other creators, share what you know, and build the relationships that bring in paid work. The estimates describe a crowded field, and the people around you are part of how you stand out in it.
