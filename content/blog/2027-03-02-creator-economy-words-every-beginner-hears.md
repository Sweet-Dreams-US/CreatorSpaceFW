If you are new to making things online, the vocabulary can feel like a second language. People talk about brand deals, monetization, professional creators and addressable markets as if everyone already knows what they mean. Most beginners do not, and that is fine.

This glossary explains the words you are most likely to hear in your first months. The definitions come from a Goldman Sachs Research article on the size of the creator economy, written in plain language and with the figures the article reports.

## Who counts as a creator

The word at the center of it all is creator. Goldman Sachs Research describes creators as individuals who have their own brands and online audiences. The definition is short, and it is broad on purpose. It does not name a medium. A photographer, a musician, a writer, a podcaster and a video maker can all fit.

Two parts of the definition do the work. A creator has a brand, which means people recognize the work and the name behind it. A creator also has an audience, which means people who follow along online. If you are building either one, you are doing what the word describes.

The same article estimates there are about 50 million creators worldwide, and it expects that number to grow at a rate of 10 to 20 percent a year over the following five years. That growth rate is called a compound annual growth rate, a measure of how much something grows each year when each year builds on the last.

:::note The number of creators
Goldman Sachs Research estimates about 50 million creators worldwide, growing at a compound annual rate of 10 to 20 percent over five years.
:::

## Influencer and influencer marketing

An influencer is a creator who promotes products to an audience. Not every creator is an influencer. A musician who shares songs is a creator. When a creator tells an audience about a product, that creator is acting as an influencer.

Influencer marketing is the spending that companies put behind this kind of promotion. The Goldman Sachs article names influencer marketing as the main driver of growth in the creator economy. When you hear someone say a company has an influencer budget, this is what they mean.

## Brand deals

A brand deal is a paid arrangement between a creator and a company that wants its product promoted. It is one of the three main routes the article describes for how creators earn money. According to survey data the article cites, brand deals are the main source of creator income, at about 70 percent.

That share is useful to know when you read about creator earnings. Much of the money in the field comes from companies paying creators for promotion, and less comes from platforms or from followers.

## Monetization

Monetization is the word for the ways a creator earns money from the work. The article describes three routes. The first is brand deals. The second is a share of advertising revenue from the platforms that host the content. The third is subscriptions, donations or other direct payments from followers.

Beginners often hear monetization used as if it were one switch that gets turned on. It is closer to a menu of options. The article notes that platforms offering several ways to earn are best placed to attract creators.

:::note Three routes to income
The article lists brand deals, a share of advertising revenue from host platforms, and subscriptions, donations or other direct payments from followers as the main ways creators earn.
:::

## Platform payouts and direct payments

Two terms sit under monetization. Platform payouts are the payments a platform makes to a creator, and the article links them to advertising on short-form video. Short-form video is exactly what it sounds like, brief clips of the kind many social apps feature.

Direct payments come straight from the audience. A subscription, a donation and a one-time payment from a follower all fit here. The creator is paid by the people who watch or read, not by a company or a platform.

## Professional creators

Goldman Sachs Research uses the term professional creators for creators who earn more than $100,000 a year. The article says they make up about 4 percent of global creators and expects that share to stay steady.

This figure is worth keeping in view. It tells a beginner that earning a full living at this level is a small slice of the field. Most creators are somewhere earlier on the path, and the word professional here is a statement about income, not about skill or effort.

## Total addressable market

The article estimates the creator economy's total addressable market at about $250 billion at the time it was written, rising to about $480 billion by 2027. Total addressable market means the total amount of money a field could bring in. It is a way of describing size, and it comes with the caution that it is an estimate.

The article says that growth is roughly in line with Goldman Sachs Research's estimates for global digital advertising spend over the same period.

The article also uses the phrase flywheel effect, which it uses to describe how small gains compound into further growth. It lists six factors behind it, which are scale, capital, AI recommendation engines, monetization tools, data and analytics, and e-commerce.

It also uses the phrase flight to quality, which describes creators moving toward stable, large platforms during economic uncertainty and rising interest rates.

You may not hear these every day, but they show up in articles about the field, and now you will know what they refer to.

## Putting the words to use

A glossary only helps if the words become familiar. The fastest way to make that happen is to hear them used by people who work in the field and to ask what they mean. Listen for the difference between a creator and an influencer. Ask how someone earns, and notice whether the answer sounds like a brand deal, a platform payout or a direct payment.
