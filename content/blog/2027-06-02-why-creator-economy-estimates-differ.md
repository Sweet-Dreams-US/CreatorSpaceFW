If you search for the size of the creator economy, you will find numbers that do not agree. One source gives a few million people. Another gives tens of millions. A third gives a figure in the hundreds of millions. This is not always a sign that someone made a mistake. In most cases the studies are counting different things.

This post uses a report from Meta, written by Richard Florida, to show why. It lists several estimates side by side, which makes the differences easy to see.

## The numbers in one place

Meta's article, published November 1, 2022, summarizes a report by Richard Florida called "The Rise of the Creator Economy." It includes these figures:

- Some exploratory studies forecast the creator economy to reach more than $100 billion.
- The report puts creators at between 30 and 85 million Americans, and an estimated 300 million people globally.
- As many as 17 million creators earned money by selling digital content as of 2017.
- Up to 60 million Americans are estimated to belong to what Florida calls the Creative Class, which the article says represents more than 40 percent of the national workforce, up from 15 percent in 1980.

Each figure comes from a different source and answers a different question. Looking at them one at a time explains most of the gap.

## Reason one: the definition of a creator

Florida defines individual creators as people who use digital technology to make and publish unique creative content that audiences can access and respond to. That is a fairly wide definition. It covers someone who posts videos for fun as well as someone who earns a full income from their work.

A narrower definition gives a smaller count. If you count only people who earn money from their content, the number drops. The 17 million figure in the article is about people who earned money by selling digital content, so it is not the same group as the 30 to 85 million creators in the other estimate.

:::note Hobbyists are part of the count
The Meta article says most creators are motivated by intrinsic rewards. It says many are hobbyists, and a growing number are activists motivated by impact rather than money, fame or follower counts. Whether a study counts those people changes its total.
:::

## Reason two: what is being measured

Some studies count people. Some count money. Some count both. The $100 billion figure is a forecast of the size of the economy. The 300 million figure is an estimate of people worldwide. These measure different things, and a larger number of people does not always mean a larger amount of money, because many creators earn little or nothing.

The Creative Class figure is different again. It includes scientists, technology workers, artists, designers, entertainers and other professional knowledge workers. That is far broader than people who post online, which is why it reaches up to 60 million Americans and more than 40 percent of the workforce.

## Reason three: the year and the source

Each estimate in the article has a date and an origin. The $100 billion forecast cites NeoReach and Influencer Marketing Hub from May 2021. The 300 million figure comes from Edelman Data & Intelligence, in a global study from August 2022. The 17 million figure comes from Re:Create, in a report from February 2019 that counts creators as of 2017.

Numbers from different years are hard to compare, because the number of people publishing online changes over time. A figure from 2017 and a figure from 2022 describe different moments. The Meta article also gives no figures for 2026, so anyone using it today should treat the numbers as dated.

## Reason four: forecasts are not counts

The $100 billion number is described in the article as a forecast from some exploratory studies. A forecast is a prediction about the future. A count is a record of people or dollars that already exist. When a headline mixes the two, the result can look like a fact when it is really an estimate.

When you read a number about the creator economy, ask whether it is a count, an estimate or a forecast. That one question clears up many apparent conflicts.

## How to read a creator economy number

Here is a simple checklist you can use on any figure you see:

1. Who counts as a creator in this study?
2. Is it counting people, money, or both?
3. What year does the number describe?
4. Who produced it, and for what purpose?
5. Is it a count, an estimate or a forecast?

Meta's article also notes that the report was written independently by Florida and that future phases are planned to develop new data and scope the global creator economy further. That is a reminder that the field is still being measured, and the numbers will keep changing.

## Why it matters for working creators

If you make creative work and you want to know how big your field is, the answer depends on which group you are in. A person who sells services to local clients is in a different market from someone who builds a large online audience, even though both may be counted as creators.

The Creator Space site lists categories that include photographers, videographers, musicians, designers, writers, developers, producers, animators, filmmakers and podcasters. ## The short version

Estimates of the creator economy differ because they use different definitions, measure different things, come from different years and sometimes mix counts with forecasts. The figures in the Meta article range from 17 million to 300 million because they describe different groups. Before you rely on any number, check who it counts and when it was measured.
