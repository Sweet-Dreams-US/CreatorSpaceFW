If you take photos, write songs, design logos, edit video or build things for a living, you have probably been told you work in a creative field. Economists have a more specific idea. They talk about a creative class, and the idea has shaped how cities think about artists, designers and other independent workers. This lesson explains where the term comes from, who it covers, and what it can and cannot tell you about your own work.

## Where the term comes from

The term was made popular by Richard Florida, an urban studies scholar and the author of *The Rise of the Creative Class*. He argued that a growing share of workers earn their living by creating new ideas, designs and content, and that these workers have an outsized effect on where economic growth happens.

Florida later wrote a report on the creator economy for Meta. In it, he describes the creative class as a group that includes scientists, technology workers, artists, designers, entertainers and professional knowledge workers in fields such as management, healthcare and law.

That is a wide definition. It covers a software engineer and a muralist, which tells you the term is about the kind of work done, not about the art world alone.

## Two groups inside the creative class

Florida divides the creative class into two main parts.

The first is what he calls the super-creative core. These are workers in science, engineering, education, computer programming and research, with a smaller set in arts, design and media. Their main job is to create and innovate.

The second is the creative professionals. These are knowledge-based workers, for example in healthcare, business and finance, law and education, who apply advanced education to solve specific problems.

A smaller group sometimes called bohemians is also part of the picture. This is where many working artists, musicians and writers are placed.

:::note Two groups, one class
In Florida's model the creative class has a super-creative core and a larger group of creative professionals, with a smaller group of bohemians alongside them.
:::

## The three Ts

Florida also asked why some cities draw creative workers and others do not. His answer is often summarized as the three Ts.

Talent means a highly educated, skilled population. Tolerance means a diverse and open community. Technology means infrastructure that supports entrepreneurship.

You can agree or disagree with the theory, and economists have argued about it for years. What is useful here is the idea behind it: creative people tend to cluster where they can find other skilled people, where they feel welcome, and where it is practical to start something.

## How big is the creative class

Estimates vary with the year, the data and the way the class is counted, so it helps to see more than one number.

The Meta report states that estimates indicate up to 60 million Americans are in the creative class, more than 40 percent of the national workforce, up from 15 percent in 1980. Another summary of Florida's work describes the class as about 40 million U.S. workers, roughly 30 percent of the workforce, with the super-creative core at about 12 percent of jobs.

The two figures differ, and that is normal. The size depends on which occupations are counted. The point is that this is a large share of working people, not a small niche.

:::note Creators are a related but separate group
The Meta report defines creators as people who use digital technology to make and publish unique creative content that audiences can access and respond to. Reviewed studies put creators at 30 to 85 million Americans, and the report attributes those figures to other studies, not to its own count.
:::

## Creative class versus creator economy

The two terms overlap but are not the same. The creative class is a way of grouping occupations, and it is broad. The creator economy is about people who publish creative content to an audience, often online.

A freelance photographer who books weddings is likely part of the creative class. A photographer who also posts tutorials and sells presets is part of the creator economy too. Many people fit both.

Florida's report notes that most creators are driven mainly by intrinsic rewards, such as making an impact, more than by money, fame or follower counts. He also sees an opportunity to build what he calls a creator middle class, where creators who choose to can earn a decent living.

## What this means for a local creative community

Put the pieces together and a practical point appears. If creative workers cluster where they can find other skilled people, then a local network of creators has value that no single freelancer can build alone. Someone needs to find the videographer, the illustrator or the musician who is already in town.

That is where a local directory and regular meetups matter. They make the talent in a city visible, which is the first step to hiring locally, collaborating and learning from each other.

## Getting more from your own network

You do not need a definition to benefit from the idea. A few habits help.

Know who is nearby. Spend time with people in neighboring skills. A designer who knows a good photographer and a good developer can offer clients more.

Show up. Free meetups give you a chance to meet people without a sales pitch.

Trade skills. Many creatives know something another creative wants to learn, and an exchange costs nothing.

Be easy to find. Keep your listing and portfolio current so that someone searching for your skill can reach you.

:::

## The short version

The creative class is a broad grouping of workers who earn a living by creating ideas, designs and content. Richard Florida divides it into a super-creative core and creative professionals, and he links its growth to talent, tolerance and technology. Counts differ depending on method, but all of them describe a large part of the workforce. For an individual creator, the most useful takeaway is simple: your work is stronger when you are connected to others who do creative work nearby.
